# Time Under Observation — Sources

This source map is intentionally mixed: primary texts/artifacts, scholarly interpretation, archaeology, linguistic critique, and modern scientific comparison are kept in separate families.

## Hindu cosmological cycles

### Primary / traditional

- **Bhagavad Gita 8.17.** A day of Brahma is described in terms of one thousand yugas, with a corresponding night. This establishes the nested cosmic-time structure but does not by itself supply a modern geochronological interpretation.
- **Puranic cosmological traditions.** Later conventional arithmetic organizes Mahayugas, manvantaras, kalpas, pralaya, and Brahma-scale durations. Exact formulations vary across texts and strata.

### Scholarly orientation

- W. J. Johnson, **A Dictionary of Hinduism**. Oxford University Press. Useful for terminology and conventional Hindu cosmological units.
- Ludo Rocher, **The Puranas**. Harrassowitz, 1986. Useful for resisting the treatment of “the Puranas” as one uniform text or date.

### Comparison control

Modern Earth-age estimates are approximately 4.54 billion years. The traditional 4.32-billion-year kalpa is numerically close but is not textually identified as a measured geological Earth age and lacks a demonstrated observational derivation comparable to modern geochronology.

## Buddhist impermanence and temporal construction

### Primary

- **Dhammapada 277** — *sabbe sankhara anicca*, conventionally translated as “all conditioned things/formations are impermanent.”

### Interpretive restraint

Later Buddhist traditions develop more specific theories of moments, causation, emptiness, and continuity. Those developments should be sourced independently rather than back-projected into one early verse.

### Modern comparison families

Relevant modern parallels include psychophysics of temporal integration, constructed perceptual continuity, memory updating, neural-state change, biological turnover, and thermodynamic irreversibility. These support only a qualitative observation that conditioned systems change; they do not make Buddhist technical vocabulary interchangeable with physics or neuroscience.

## Aboriginal Australian deep-time memory and “everywhen”

- Patrick D. Nunn and Nicholas J. Reid, **“Aboriginal Memories of Inundation of the Australian Coast Dating from More than 7000 Years Ago.”** *Australian Geographer* 47(1), 2016, 11–47. DOI: **10.1080/00049182.2015.1077539**. The paper examines coastal-inundation stories from 21 locations and compares geographic details with postglacial sea-level histories, deriving possible ages of approximately 7,250–13,070 calibrated years BP for the relevant observations.
- W. E. H. Stanner, **“The Dreaming.”** Originally developed in mid-20th-century anthropological work and later collected in *White Man Got No Dreaming*. Source of the influential “everywhen” formulation. Stanner explicitly emphasizes the difficulty of translating the underlying concepts into ordinary Western temporal categories.
- Ann McGrath, Laura Rademaker, and Jakelin Troy, eds., **Everywhen: Australia and the Language of Deep History.** A useful modern collection for approaching deep history and Indigenous temporal frameworks without reducing them to one simple “Dreamtime” doctrine.

### Caution

“Dreaming,” “Dreamtime,” and “everywhen” are English-language interpretive terms operating across many distinct Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. No single universal Indigenous physics of time is claimed here.

## Maya calendrics and Venus

### Primary artifact

- **Dresden Codex**, especially the Venus table on pages 24 and 46–50. The table uses a 584-day integer approximation to the Venus synodic cycle.

### Scholarship

- Anthony F. Aveni, **Skywatchers** (rev. ed.). University of Texas Press, 2001. Major archaeoastronomical treatment of Mesoamerican observation and calendrics.
- Harvey M. Bricker and Victoria R. Bricker, **Astronomy in the Maya Codices.** American Philosophical Society, 2011. Detailed study of astronomical tables in the surviving Maya codices.

### Comparison control

The modern mean Venus synodic period is approximately **583.92 days**. A 584-day calendrical value is therefore a strong specimen of successful observational compression. That accuracy is independent of and does not validate modern 2012 catastrophe interpretations.

## Hopi migration, calendrics, and temporal language

### Recorded tradition / mediated source

- Frank Waters, with drawings and source material recorded by Oswald White Bear Fredericks, **Book of the Hopi.** Viking Press, 1963. Part Two contains “The Four Migrations” and **“North to the Back Door.”** The phrase is therefore sourceable, but the book is mediated and should not be treated as a transparent or universally authoritative transcript of Hopi tradition.
- Edmond Nequatewa, **Truth of a Hopi: Stories Relating to the Origin, Myths, and Clan Histories of the Hopi.** Useful as an additional Hopi-authored/recorded source family for emergence and clan-history material.

### Archaeology + oral tradition

- Patrick D. Lyons, **Ancestral Hopi Migrations.** Anthropological Papers of the University of Arizona 68, University of Arizona Press, 2003. Uses ceramic sourcing, settlement archaeology, and oral tradition to investigate ancestral Hopi population movements within the Southwest. This is a materially constrained control specimen for migration-memory convergence.

### Linguistics

- Benjamin Lee Whorf, writings on Hopi and linguistic relativity, especially his treatment of temporal categories. Important historically, but frequently simplified in popular retellings.
- Ekkehart Malotki, **Hopi Time: A Linguistic Analysis of the Temporal Concepts in the Hopi Language.** Mouton, 1983. Extensive documentation of temporal metaphor, units of time, horizon-based sun time, stellar orientation, ceremonial calendars, and timekeeping devices. Strong counterevidence to the slogan that Hopi “has no concept of time.”

## Modern scientific control families

These sources are comparison controls, not validators of traditional metaphysics:

- radiometric geochronology and modern estimates of Earth age;
- postglacial sea-level reconstruction;
- modern orbital astronomy of Venus and lunisolar cycles;
- chronobiology and biological oscillators;
- psychophysics and neuroscience of duration, temporal binding, perceptual integration, memory, and subjective continuity;
- thermodynamic irreversibility and entropy.

## Source-quality rule for this case

When a correspondence looks exciting, ask four separate questions:

1. **Is the traditional claim actually in an identifiable source?**
2. **Could the underlying phenomenon have been directly observed?**
3. **Is the modern resemblance structural, numerical, causal, or merely verbal?**
4. **Can we show a measurement/inference path, or are we supplying it retrospectively?**

Only the fourth question can turn a striking resemblance into evidence that an older tradition possessed the corresponding modern empirical knowledge.
